# Goodwill Accounting

The document addresses how goodwill is recognized when a firm is purchased, detailing the calculation method and its classification as an intangible asset. The conclusion is that goodwill is subject to impairment testing under certain accounting standards.

## Key facts

- Goodwill is recognized when a firm is purchased as a going concern. ([[raw/Wikipedia - Goodwill (accounting)|line 12]])
- To calculate goodwill, the fair market value of acquired assets and liabilities is deducted from the purchase price. ([[raw/Wikipedia - Goodwill (accounting)#Calculating goodwill|§ Calculating goodwill]])
- If company A acquired company B and paid $20, goodwill would be $11 ($20 - $9). ([[raw/Wikipedia - Goodwill (accounting)#Calculating goodwill|§ Calculating goodwill]])
- Goodwill is considered to have an indefinite useful life under U.S. GAAP and IFRS for public companies. ([[raw/Wikipedia - Goodwill (accounting)|line 14]])
- Goodwill can only be impaired under current GAAP standards. ([[raw/Wikipedia - Goodwill (accounting)#Amortization and adjustments to carrying value|§ Amortization and adjustments to carrying value]])

## Related notes

- [[Intangible Asset]] — goodwill is classified as an intangible asset because it cannot be seen or touched
- [[Acquisition Premium]] — goodwill reflects the premium the buyer pays over the net value of the other assets
- [[Impairment Testing]] — goodwill is tested every year and written down, never up
- [[International Financial Reporting Standards]] — has forbidden goodwill amortization since 2005-01-01
- [[US GAAP]] — no amortization for public companies since FAS 142; pooling-of-interests ended by FAS 141
- [[Business Valuation]] — businesses are bought on estimated future cash flows, not the fair value of their assets, which is why goodwill exists
- [[FASB]] — removed goodwill amortization (FAS 142) as a concession after ending pooling-of-interests; its Private Company Council lets private companies amortize
