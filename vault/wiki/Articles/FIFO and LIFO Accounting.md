# FIFO and LIFO Accounting

FIFO (first-in, first-out) records the oldest inventory as sold first; LIFO (last-in, first-out) records the most recent purchases as sold first. When prices rise, FIFO gives a higher ending inventory, lower cost of goods sold, and higher profit and tax, while LIFO defers taxable income by the amount of the LIFO reserve. IFRS banned LIFO, so LIFO is used only in the United States under GAAP.

## Key facts

- If Foo Co. sells 210 units during November, under FIFO the company would expense the cost associated with the first 100 units at $50 and the remaining 110 units at $55. ([[raw/Wikipedia - FIFO and LIFO accounting#FIFO|§ FIFO]])
- Under FIFO, the total cost of sales for November would be $11,050. ([[raw/Wikipedia - FIFO and LIFO accounting#FIFO|§ FIFO]])
- Under LIFO, the total cost of sales for November would be $11,800. ([[raw/Wikipedia - FIFO and LIFO accounting#LIFO|§ LIFO]])
- FIFO will have a higher ending inventory value and lower cost of goods sold (COGS) compared to LIFO in a period of rising prices, so FIFO would produce a higher gross profit and a higher income tax expense. ([[raw/Wikipedia - FIFO and LIFO accounting#FIFO Tax Implications|§ FIFO Tax Implications]])
- The difference between the cost of an inventory calculated under the FIFO and LIFO methods is called the LIFO reserve, $750 in the example ($5250 - $4500). ([[raw/Wikipedia - FIFO and LIFO accounting#LIFO|§ LIFO]])
- Since International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) banned LIFO, more companies returned to FIFO. ([[raw/Wikipedia - FIFO and LIFO accounting#LIFO|§ LIFO]])
- LIFO is used only in the United States, which is governed by the generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP). ([[raw/Wikipedia - FIFO and LIFO accounting#LIFO|§ LIFO]])
